## The call

I work with AI every day. This week, it called me.

A blocked number rang my cell. My iPhone screens unknown callers, so I watched the message get transcribed as he talked. It was the sheriff's department. I figured I'd better take that one, so I answered.

He said he was a deputy with the OH-bee-on County Sheriff's Office. Good ole southern accent, polite, the kind of voice that could turn mean if it needed to. He sounded like the sheriff from just about any made-for-TV movie.

He told me a bench warrant had been issued for my arrest because I missed jury duty. My summons had been served, he said, and he pulled it up on his computer. I could hear the keys clicking while he looked.

That's when I spoke up. I told him I'd heard of a scam like this and asked who he was with. Same answer, same pronunciation. I told him that was doubtful, since he couldn't even say the name of my county. He hung up immediately.

## How I knew it wasn't a person

The county name got my attention, but it wasn't until after he hung up that I put the rest together.

First, the keyboard. It sounded right at first, but looking back it was too even. No pauses, no hunting for a key, just a sound effect playing under the voice.

Second, the way he talked. He sounded like a sheriff, honestly a better sheriff than most sheriffs. Every sentence came out clean. No "uh," no restarts, no losing his place. Real people stumble, especially when they're reading a file and talking at the same time. He never did.

Put those together and I'm fairly sure I wasn't talking to a person at all. I was talking to software reading a script, with a voice built to sound like the guy you'd least want to argue with.

I can't prove it. But I spend my days building with these tools, and that call had the same polish I hear from them every day.

## Why you'll get one too

The jury duty warrant scam is old. What's new is how cheap it is to run.

A few years ago, a call like mine needed a real person with a decent accent and a script, working one number at a time. Now software can do the talking. Pick a voice, load a script, feed it a list of numbers. One operator can run thousands of these calls a day, and the voice never gets tired or nervous.

The numbers back this up. The FTC says Americans reported losing [$3.5 billion to imposter scams in 2025](https://ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/06/ftc-data-show-people-reported-losing-3-point-5-billion-imposter-scams-2025), and about $920 million of that was people pretending to be government, up from $789 million the year before. The FBI warned in May 2025 that scammers were sending [AI-generated voice messages](https://www.ic3.gov/PSA/2025/PSA250515) posing as senior US officials. And sheriff's offices keep issuing the same warning about this exact jury duty call, [as Columbus County, North Carolina did last month](https://www.wect.com/2026/09/22/scam-alert-sheriffs-office-warns-residents-jury-duty-warrant-phone-scams/).

Nobody can tell you yet what share of these calls are AI. My guess is it's more than people think, and growing.

## What I'd tell any business owner

You don't need to spot the AI. You need a few habits that work no matter who, or what, is on the line.

- **Know what courts don't do.** The federal court in Iowa [spells it out](https://www.iand.uscourts.gov/jury-duty-scams): it doesn't call to say a warrant was issued, it doesn't take money to make an arrest go away, and fines for skipping jury duty only come after an in-person hearing before a judge. Local courts work the same way.
- **Hang up and call them yourself.** Look up the sheriff's office or the bank on your own. Never use a number the caller gives you.
- **Screen unknown callers.** iPhone and Android can both answer unknown numbers for you and show what the caller says before you pick up. Seeing "sheriff's department" on my screen is what got me to answer, so read it with some suspicion too.
- **Give them nothing.** Not your date of birth, not a "yes," not even the right way to say your county.
- **Set an office rule.** Anyone who gets an urgent call about money, a warrant, or a payment change hangs up and checks with you first. Some families and small offices use a code word for real emergencies. It sounds silly until you need it.
- **Report it.** A quick call to your actual sheriff's office helps them warn everybody else. You can also file at [reportfraud.ftc.gov](https://reportfraud.ftc.gov).

## The advantage of a hard name

The software that called me was good. Good voice, good script, good sound effects. What it didn't have was any idea where I live.

That's the gap these calls still have, for now. They know your number and maybe your name. They don't know your town, your sheriff, or how folks around here say things. When a caller gets one of those wrong, believe it.

And no, I'm not telling him how to say it.

Living in a county nobody can pronounce has its advantages.
